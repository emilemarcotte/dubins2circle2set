\subsection{Cost function}

The cost function is
\begin{align}
    J = \underset{u(t)}{\min}\int_{t_0}^{t_f} 1dt
\end{align}
where $t_0$ is the initial time, and $t_f$ is the time at which the vehicle arrived at $(x_f,y_f)$.

Although the objective is to find the fastest path, since the vehicle travels at a constant speed and is assumed to be in a homogeneous environment (friction and drag are constant, and no external perturbations), finding the fastest path is equivalent to finding the shortest path.

\subsection{Boundary conditions}

The point $(x_g,y_g) \in \mathcal{C}$ is reached by the vehicle at time $t_g\in (t_0,t_f)$. This means the following state equality needs to be satisfied
\begin{align}
    N_\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}(t_g)) = (x(t_g)-x_c)^2 + (y(t_g)-y_c)^2 - r_c^2 = 0.
\end{align}

At the final destination, the point $(x_f,y_f)\in\mathcal{X}$ is reached at time $t_f >t_g$. This means the following state equality needs to be satisfied
\begin{align}
    N_\mathcal{X}(\mathbf{x}(t_f)) = (x(t_f)-x_x)^2 + (y(t_f)-y_x)^2 - r_x^2 = 0.
\end{align}

\subsection{What needs to be determined}

The intersection points $(x_g,y_g)$ and $(x_f,y_f)$ need to be determined so that they respect the equality constraints and minimize the Hamiltonian. The heading angles at these locations, respectively $\theta_g$ and $\theta_f$, also need to be determined.



\subsection{Hamiltonian and costates}

Create the Hamiltonian for the stated problem
\begin{align}
    H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},u) = 1 + p_xv_0\cos\theta + p_yv_0\sin\theta + p_\theta u
\end{align}
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{\dot{p}} = \begin{bmatrix}
        \dot{p}_x\\\dot{p}_y\\\dot{p}_\theta
    \end{bmatrix}
    = -\pdv{H}{\mathbf{x}}
    = \begin{bmatrix}
        0\\0\\-p_xv_0\sin\theta + p_yv_0\cos\theta
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix}
        0\\0\\-p_x \dot{y}(t) + p_y\dot{x}(t)
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
where $p_x$, $p_y$ and $p_\theta$ are the costates of the system.

Since the derivatives of $p_x$ and $p_y$ are equal to zero, it means they are constant and do not depend on time. This will, however, not be the case for $p_\theta$. By integrating $\dot{p}_\theta$, we obtain
\begin{align}
    p_\theta(t) = \int \dot{p}_\theta(t)dt
    = \int (-p_x \dot{y}(t) + p_y\dot{x}(t))dt
    = -p_x y(t) + p_yx(t) + c_1
    \label{eq_ptheta}
\end{align}
where $c_1$ is a constant of integration.

According to the minimum principle, the optimal control $u^*$ will be given by
\begin{align}
    u^* = \underset{ u \in \left[-\frac{v_0}{\rho},\frac{v_0}{\rho}\right] }{\arg\min} \; H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},u).
\end{align}

Since the only term that depends on $u$ in the Hamiltonian is multiplied by $p_\theta$, the value of $p_\theta$ will help dictate the optimal control $u^*$. First, if $p_\theta=0$, the following result is obtained
\begin{align}
    p_\theta(t) &= -p_x y(t) + p_yx(t) + c_1 = 0\\
    y(t) &= \frac{p_x}{p_y}x(t) + \frac{c_1}{p_y}.
\end{align}
This is the equation of a straight line. For the vehicle to travel this way, its angular velocity must be equal to 0, which means $u=0$. Whenever $p_\theta$ takes non-zero values, it follows directly from the Hamiltonian that $u^*$ will take either its maximum or minimum value depending on the sign of $p_\theta$.
\begin{align}
    u^* = \begin{cases}
        -v_0/\rho & p_\theta(t) > 0\\
        0 & p_\theta(t) = 0\\
        v_0/\rho & p_\theta(t) < 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{align}

The resulting optimal control is a "bang-off-bang" control.



\subsection{Optimal path characterization}

According to \cite{Dubins57}, the optimal path of a vehicle with bound curvature and constant forward speed going from one given point to another can take two different forms : CCC or CSC, where C represents a left (L) or right (R) turn (curved segment), while S represents a straight segment, and two consecutive segments cannot be of the same type. This means there are six different possible trajectories : LRL, RLR, LSR, LSL, RSL, RSR. It should be noted that any segment from those trajectories could be of length zero.

According to the optimality principle in \cite{Bellman1966}, it is possible to state that the global optimal path will be a concatenation of two Dubins trajectories, one of those giving the optimal trajectory before the tangent point $(x_g,y_g)$, and the other one giving the optimal trajectory after the tangent point. If we use the symbol "|" to represent the concatenation of two segments, this means the optimal trajectory will either take the form CCC|CCC, CCC|CSC, CSC|CCC, or CSC|CSC. This means there are 36 possible types of trajectories.

\section{Doing LSLS case with free final angle}

Since the final angle is free, it will be dictated by the tangent line leaving the turn radius of $c_1$, which means the final segment will always be a straight line.

We assume the final point is at least a distance $2\rho$ away from the circle $\mathcal{C}$, so that it is impossible to have it inside circle $c_1$.
\begin{align*}
    q &= \tan\frac{\theta_1}{2}\\
    \cos\theta_1 &= \frac{1-\tan^2(\theta_1/2)}{1+\tan^2(\theta_1/2)}
    = \frac{1-q^2}{1+q^2}\\
    \sin\theta_1 &= \frac{2\tan(\theta_1/2)}{1+\tan^2(\theta_1/2)}
    = \frac{2q}{1+q^2}
\end{align*}
\begin{align}
